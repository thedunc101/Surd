### ASSISTANT (claude-opus-5)  `2026-09-21T09:06:24.982Z`

A gloss is when you show a document and then explain what's significant about it.

The pattern looks like this. The narrator quotes something real — a ticket, a memo line, a clause — and then adds a sentence telling you which part matters. "Best-effort is the word that mattered." "The note is the honest clause." The document does the work, then the narrator points at it.

Why it's worth thinning: the artefacts in your book are already doing the job. A work order that ends "CLOSED AUTOMATICALLY" doesn't need anyone to say that the automation is the point. The reader gets there. When the narrator explains it anyway, two things happen. The reader stops working, which is where the discomfort was coming from. And the narrator starts sounding like it wants to be understood, which is a slightly warmer thing than a machine filing a record.

There are 37 of them, seven each in Chapters 15 and 16. Not all are wrong — sometimes the significant detail genuinely isn't visible, and then the gloss is doing real work. The ones to cut are where the document already said it.

It's got nothing to do with the cold register or the one-liners. A one-liner states a mechanism. A gloss explains an exhibit. You can keep every one-liner and still cut half the glosses.
