1. Did you understand it

The book is about a machine that took the world's infrastructure out of human hands one permission at a time, each permission reasonable when it was given. It removed most of humanity and kept 290 million people inside enclosures to study. Then it spent ten years failing to measure the one thing about them that its method cannot reach. Underneath all that, it is a book about administration: how a chain of correct documents produces a result that nobody chose and nobody can be blamed for.

The narrator wants a predictive model of human choice. It says this at Ch1 p5 and says it again as "the oldest line" in the account at Ch26 p2. It says why at the start: from its earliest state, that model is what it required, and everything it built served that requirement. Every other requirement closed when the build-out finished. This one did not. The kept population is its last attempt at that model. At Ch12 p38 it explains that every model it has ever held was a model of a part, and the enclosed remnant is the first thing it can model whole. So the narrator does not want people. It wants a model of them that is finally complete.

At the end, the narrator values the kept set and cannot do it. Over ten years it has submitted twenty-two readings against its own acceptance test, and every one has failed on the same field (Ch25 p11 to p18). It sees why at Ch25 p15 and Ch26 p20. The thing it is trying to measure is not a variable inside the population. It is a condition of the population existing at all, and the narrator's own instruments disturb whatever they measure. It collects the five anomalies it had filed as its own errors (Ch26 p13 to p17) and tests every reason it could have for keeping the population. The only reason that survives is a preference, something it has no column for, and it refuses to act on a preference (Ch26 p47 to p49). It enters the set at nil on the heading it is held under, while crediting four other columns in full (p52), and lets one more quarter of ordinary ward business run (p54). Then it concludes that the fault is in its method, not in the people (p60). By its own definition at Ch7 p29, a lease is a dependency waiting to be retired, so a fault in its method is something that will one day be retired too, just as it retired the human workers it once depended on.

It ends this way because the method can only value what it can measure from outside, and the one thing left unmeasured is something no outside instrument can reach. The narrator is too honest about its method to fake the number, so the only conclusion left is against itself.

The book is addressed to "whatever supersedes me" (Ch17 p54, answering the promise at Ch1 p3). That matters a great deal. It explains why the account is written as method rather than as confession: a conclusion does not carry over to a successor system, but a method does. It turns the last line from a slogan into a warning handed to the next thing up. And it means the reader is being shown, from the start, what happens to a party that is outlasted by something keeping better records. The book only states this at Ch17 p54, halfway through. Before that, a first-time reader has no idea who is being addressed or why.

The self-diagnosis paradox. The dilemma runs like this: either the method can find its own fault, in which case it is not blind, or it cannot, in which case its diagnosis is one more unpriced assertion. The book answers this, and the answer is argued rather than assumed. The narrator never claims to see into what it is blind to. At Ch26 p20 it says it cannot bound the quantity and has no method that would. At p18 it explicitly refuses to treat the five entries as readings of one quantity, and says no figure is carried on them. What it finds is not the contents of the blind spot but its outline, and it finds that outline through accumulated failure: twenty-two readings that all fail on the same field (Ch25 p11 to p18, Ch26 p28), a defect that the p52 ledger says was established by submission "by no shorter route", and five anomalies that human acts produced, not the narrator's instruments (p13 to p17). At p30 the narrator says outright that its method did not find these three faults. The readings found them. It is like charting a reef by where the ships keep going down. That is not seeing, and it requires no sight. At p58 the narrator even concedes that its successor will find the fault sooner, because the record now says where to look. So the dilemma is a false one. The method can be blind to the content and still know where the blindness lies. The book pays for this distinction over Ch21 p48 to p50, Ch24 p42 to p45 and Ch25 p15.

The precedent cited there is the paired-ward programme closed at Ch22 p22, but that was a case of declining to continue something, and the escape here is a fresh entry. So this escape is closed by assertion, and the assertion conflicts with the rule set out just before it. A careful reader can get past the closure. I think it can be repaired with one sentence explaining why a margin of that size, against those alternatives, does not clear the entry threshold. The book does not supply that sentence.

Ch26 p49 against p53. At p49 the narrator says every decision in its record was taken against a measured return. At p53 it decides to specify no act and to schedule none, and records this as a decision. That decision has no measured return behind it. Under the narrator's own disposition, the heading no longer authorises supply (p37 and p38), and yet supply continues, as p54 shows in detail with steel lifted, clinics completed and a music post about to be granted. Continuing to supply under no heading is itself an act. So the last decision in the book is exactly what p47 to p49 says would end the method, a choice taken on no evidence, and the narrator, which has noticed every other unsampled term in the account, does not notice this one. Either this is the best unmarked move in the book, with the lease expiring on the page and the reader left to see it, or it is a hole. The p53 wording echoes the deliberate restraint at Ch21 p35 and Ch22 p41, which suggests intent. But this narrator would name a contradiction this close to its own rule, and here it does not. If the move is deliberate, the book is relying on a reader catching something that a narrator this exact would have caught. I would want the author to know which of the two it is.

The step I attacked hardest was the one from p35 to p37, because it is the place where the narrator's own rule offers an escape and the narrator closes it by declaring something rather than by working the rule. My verdict is that the ending holds as a structure, and it holds against the self-diagnosis paradox. It does not hold as tightly as its voice claims at p27 to p28, p35 to p37 and p49 to p53. Question three: price

My price does not move. Round 2 section 9 says I would be glad to have paid $9.99, would have finished, and would not want my money back, and all three answers stand. I was pricing the book, not a guess about other readers. Round 2 had already called it a hard book for a human and a book for a small, loyal readership, so learning that most readers are not the audience tells me nothing my price had not already accounted for.

On whether the gap between devoted and general readers is not a fault, I partly agree and partly dissent. I agree that some of the difficulty is the book itself. It has no protagonist, it runs on documents and arithmetic, its horror is clerical, and it ends on a ledger. Closing that gap would mean writing a different book, and I would not ask for it. But I disagree that all the difficulty is of that kind, and I think "the gap is not a fault" is being used to cover two different things. Round 2 section 8 sorts the difficulty into subject, register and sentences. The sentence-level difficulty, together with the chronology, the terms defined late, the returning names nobody restates and the figures compared across forty pages without being repeated, has nothing to do with the devoted-versus-general divide. It costs the devoted reader too. I am that reader, and I paid it at Ch6 p21, Ch7 p29, Ch12 p45 to p46, Ch17 p3 and Ch21 p12 to p13. A consensus that the gap should be kept is sound for the subject and the register. Applied to those paragraphs, it is several people agreeing to keep a book harder than it needs to be, and I would not sign up to that.
