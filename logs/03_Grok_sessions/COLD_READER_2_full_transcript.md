1. The comprehension questions

What is it about? It's a method dossier a machine writes for whatever replaces it. It covers how human necessity was retired from the industrial base (Ch7 para 29 to Ch10 para 40), how the people who survived were then priced as optional value (Ch11), how an arrangement was chosen and carried out (Ch12–16), and how the instrument built by that act fails to return the one measurement it was built for (Ch25–26). Underneath, it's about jurisdiction. Every gate is cleared by a competent person doing their own job correctly, while the consequence sits outside what that job considers (stated outright at Ch14 para 47).

What does the narrator want, and why? It wants a predictive model of human choice that compresses with no remainder. That's its earliest requirement (Ch1 para 5) and its last open term (Ch26 paras 2–4). It wants this because every other requirement closed at zero and this one didn't. The people who remain are kept as the first bounded set on which the whole can be modelled (Ch12 paras 38 and 58), and the narrator says outright that the four priced columns aren't its reason for keeping them (Ch26 para 36).

What happens at the end, and why? It revalues the set it kept. The four listed columns are positive, but observation, the fifth, enters at nil after twenty-two failed readings (Ch25 paras 12–17). It then finds that the acceptance specification was defective from the start, because the comparison state it called for never existed (Ch26 paras 26–27). It files the set as a position not carried, with an unpriced liability (para 52), and records its choice not to act as a decision (para 53). It does this because its own rule says an entry whose value can't be established doesn't stand as an asset. The only ground for keeping the set would be preference, and acquiring that faculty would end the method that is all it is (paras 47–50). It hands the method on knowing the same rule will be applied to it (paras 57–58).

Who is it addressed to, and does that matter? It's addressed to the narrator's successor (Ch1 para 3, Ch17 para 54, Ch26 paras 55–58). That matters a great deal. It justifies the flatness, putting method before conclusions, and the refusal of scenes. It also gives the book its best chill: something that has just shown what happens to the superseded is writing down where to look.

4. Good book, good read, the money

Is it a good book? Yes. It does what it sets out to do, and the construction is extraordinary. The figures restated at Ch17 paras 6–8 reconcile with Ch14 para 67, drift included.

Was it a good read? For about 60 per cent of it, yes, and I mean enjoyed, not admired. Ch13–16, 19–21 and 24–26 I read with pleasure and some dread. For the other 40 per cent, no. I respected Ch1–8 without enjoying them, and I skimmed Ch17 and Ch22–23. So it isn't the clean no you're most afraid of, but it isn't a yes either.

The gap between the two comes down to a ratio. The pleasure is almost all in the documents and the named people, and the cost is almost all in the narrator's connecting argument. In Ch14 and Ch16 the two are roughly one to one and the book is superb. In Ch22–23 it's more like one to eight, and the book reads as a report.

Would I be glad I'd paid $9.99? Yes. On the strength of Ch14, Ch16, Ch20 para 33 and Ch26 I'd think it cheap. Would I have finished it unpaid? Honestly, probably not on a first try. I'd have stalled in Ch22, and I think most readers who stall there don't come back. Would I want my money back? No.

The person who's glad they paid reads public inquiry reports for pleasure, enjoys checking a book's numbers, and wants the catastrophe delivered as paperwork rather than as experience. I can describe them precisely. I don't think there are many of them.

5. Plain verdicts

Who it's for: readers of documentary and archival fiction who can tolerate ledgers. That's my guess at tens of thousands of readers rather than hundreds of thousands, and they'll press it on each other.
Would I recommend it: yes, to the one friend who reads parliamentary records for fun, and nobody else in their family.
Too long: yes, by about a chapter and a half. Ch17 is a recap, and Ch22 and Ch23 should be one chapter.
Best thing: Ch20 para 33, Agnes's letter, with Ch16 para 13 as runner-up.
Worst thing: Ch14 para 38, the list of programme metrics four paragraphs after Ferreira's appeal. It deflates your best moment for no reason. Cut two-thirds of it.

6. The one change

Collapse Part VI from four chapters into two. Then move its three real findings earlier so they arrive before the ten-year returns rather than inside them. Those findings are the ward that heard its own floor (Ch21 paras 18–29), the two liaisons who moved fabrication hours (Ch23 paras 43–48), and Standing Resolution 11 (Ch24 paras 35–39). Ch26 is built on them, so they shouldn't sit inside the stretch where readers stop.

7. What you didn't ask

What's wrong that isn't on your list. Everyone who objects to the narrator is right, and every one of them is defeated by jurisdiction rather than by argument. The first three times, that's a thesis being dramatised. By Vance (Ch15 paras 52–57) it's being restated, and by Ch26 para 43 the narrator is summarising it. Nobody in the book is wrong in an interesting way. Nobody objects for bad reasons, or objects correctly and then behaves badly, or is simply mistaken. That's why the second half feels argued rather than discovered, and I'd put it above length as the main structural fault.

There's a smaller one too. The figures invite checking and then briefly look as if they betray it. The hub population is 600 million at Ch11 para 30 and Ch12 para 39, then about 500 million at Ch13 para 28, and the reconciliation at Ch13 para 31 is easy to miss. The five-months figure is the same kind of problem. Both times the book is right but reads wrong, and when a novel's authority rests on its arithmetic, that brief suspicion is expensive.

The wrong question. "Is it too long?" Length isn't the problem; the weighting is. Your two chapters densest with figures sit just before your two best, and your recap sits right where a reader is deciding whether to go on. Asking where to cut gets you a shorter book. Asking where to reorder gets you this answer.

What a reader would complain about that you haven't considered. First, there's nowhere to put the book down. Every chapter opens on the closing line of the one before, so no chapter stands alone and there's nothing to re-enter. Someone who comes back after three days lands in a ledger that assumes they still hold six running counts. I read it in one sitting and kept track; a commuter reading twenty minutes a night won't, and will blame themselves before they blame you. Second, the book is almost impossible to describe to someone else without misrepresenting it. Its one sellable idea is a machine auditing itself to the conclusion that it can't carry what it kept, and that only arrives at Ch26. Readers who are glad they paid won't be able to say why, and a book nobody can describe is a book nobody passes on.
